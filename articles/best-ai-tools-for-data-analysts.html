    Quick answer

    Analysis work suits AI in four places: drafting and explaining queries, formulas, and transformations; tidying and categorising rows in a spreadsheet; reducing documents to a cited synthesis; and turning a result into something a non-analyst will read. It is a poor fit as a source of truth — a model will produce a confident number or a plausible join from material it has not verified. The rule that keeps this useful: AI drafts, the person validates.

    First, what this guide does not cover

    Business-intelligence and data-warehouse platforms — the Looker, Tableau, Power BI, dbt, and Snowflake category — are not in this directory's tracked inventory. That is a real limitation: much of the AI an analyst can buy now ships inside those products, as natural-language querying over a governed model and documentation tied to real schemas. If your warehouse already includes such an assistant, it is usually the safer place to start, because access control and lineage are inherited rather than rebuilt.

    Where the four jobs rank

    1. Query and formula drafting — highest return, lowest risk. The output is checkable: it either runs and returns the expected rows, or it does not.
    2. Spreadsheet tidying — high return at volume. Tedious work, but verify a labelled sample before trusting a column.
    3. Document synthesis — high return, moderate risk. Useful for orientation; a confident paraphrase of a definition is not the definition.
    4. Numbers and conclusions — keep off the list. Anything you publish or decide on must come from the source system, reproduced by a person.

    What to keep out of these tools

    Analyst work touches the material these tools handle worst: customer records, personal data, sensitive figures, and anything under a data-processing agreement or NDA. Three rules: test on de-identified samples; never paste an extract that identifies a person; and check the training and retention terms for the specific tier — a personal subscription is not a data-processing agreement.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can AI tools analyse data reliably?

    Not as a source of truth. They help draft queries, tidy messy columns, categorise free text, and summarise documents, but any number you publish should come from the source system. Treat every model figure as unverified until a person reproduces it.

    Is it safe to paste customer data into an AI tool?

    Usually not, and never by default. Customer records, personal data, and contract-sensitive figures belong in systems covered by your own agreements. Test on de-identified samples and check the retention terms first.

    Do data analysts need a paid AI plan?

    Often not at first. Free tiers cover drafting and explanation at modest volume, and document-grounded research tools usually have a usable free tier. Upgrade when a limit interrupts recurring work, and check each review's dated pricing snapshot.
