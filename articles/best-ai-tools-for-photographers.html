    Quick answer

    Photographers get real return from AI in four places: retouching frames you already shot, extending a frame instead of reshooting, exploring concepts before a shoot, and producing the client-facing material around a gallery. Each pick is scored on workflow fit, not demo appeal. None of them decides what a client is told about how an image was made; that stays with you.

    Decide the disclosure policy before the tool

    Retouching has been normal for decades. Generative fill that adds or removes a person, an object, or a background sits on a different line, and the terms that matter are the client's and the publisher's, not the vendor's. A workable rule: change exposure, colour, dust, and crop freely; anything that alters what was in front of the lens gets logged per job, and declared where the client's terms require it. AI image rights and commercial use covers licensing in more detail, and nothing on this page is legal advice.

    The shortlist, by job

    Adobe Firefly

    4.4/5 editorial

    Generative fill and expand inside the Adobe workflow most photographers already pay for, positioned on licensed training data and commercial use. The practical win is extending a frame for a crop you did not shoot for, or removing a cable without a manual patch job. Its weakness is range: for a stylised concept image it is behind the dedicated generators.

    What to leave off the list for now

    • Fully synthetic hero imagery for commercial clients. Not because the output is bad, but because the client's disclosure terms decide whether it is usable, and that conversation is easier before the shoot than after delivery.
    • AI culling and selection. Promising and widely used, but no tool in this directory has been reviewed for it yet — treat any recommendation you read elsewhere as a trial, not a decision.
    • Any upscaler without a published rights statement. If the terms do not say what you may do with the output, you do not have the rights you think you paid for.

    What to buy first

    Order by hours saved per shoot: retouching first if portraits are the volume, frame expansion first if reshoots are expensive, concept work first if your bottleneck is winning the job rather than finishing it. Run one paid month against real client files before any annual commitment, and check the credit model: a per-seat plan with a credit cap behaves differently from a flat subscription in a busy week.     Frequently asked questions

    Can I use AI-generated images in client work?

    Sometimes, and the deciding terms belong to the client and the publisher rather than the tool. A paid plan's commercial grant is the vendor's permission to you; it does not override a contract that requires disclosure or forbids synthetic imagery.

    Is AI retouching allowed in photojournalism?

    Generally not beyond basic tone and crop. Adding or removing content in a news image breaks the record, and most outlets treat it as fabrication whatever tool produced it.

    Which AI tool should a photographer pay for first?

    Whichever removes the most repetitive hour from your week, which is usually retouching or frame expansion rather than image generation. Run one paid month on real files before an annual plan.
