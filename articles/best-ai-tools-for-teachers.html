Best free AI tools for teachers

A free-tier-first guide for educators: what each tool is genuinely good at in a classroom context, and where it falls short.

Editorial focus: tools that are useful on a $0 plan, because teacher budgets are real.

Quick answer

You don't need paid subscriptions to get real value from AI as a teacher. Five tools cover most classroom needs on their free tiers: ChatGPT for planning and drafting, Gamma for slides, Otter.ai for meeting notes, Perplexity for sourced research, and Canva AI for classroom visuals. Every tool below has a free tier at the time of writing, but free-plan limits change often — always check the tool's current pricing page before relying on it.

ChatGPT — the everyday planning assistant

The most flexible starting point. Teachers use it to draft lesson plans at multiple reading levels, generate discussion questions, write rubric language, reword parent emails into a warmer tone, and create differentiated versions of the same assignment. The honest caveat: free-tier usage limits mean heavy days can hit a cap, and outputs need fact-checking like any source. Start with our prompting basics guide — clear prompts matter more than which assistant you pick.

Gamma — fast decks and handouts

Type or paste an outline and Gamma turns it into a presentable slide deck or one-page document. It's strongest as a first-draft machine: you'll usually want to fix examples so they fit your actual students rather than generic ones, and some layouts feel templated after repeated use. Good for when you need tomorrow's deck tonight.

Otter.ai — meetings without scribbles

Otter records and transcribes staff meetings, IEP-related meetings (where policy allows recording), and professional development sessions, then produces summaries and action items. It means you can actually participate instead of transcribing. Caveats worth knowing: transcription accuracy drops with accents, crosstalk, and subject jargon, and many schools require consent before recording — check your district's rules first. See our Otter.ai review.

Perplexity — research with visible sources

When you need quick, citable background — the history of a scientific discovery, current statistics for a debate unit, context on a novel's setting — Perplexity answers with links attached, which makes verification part of the workflow instead of an afterthought. That citation habit is also exactly what we want students to learn. Free tier covers casual use; limits apply.

Canva AI — classroom visuals

Inside the editor many teachers already use, Canva's AI features speed up posters, worksheet headers, newsletter layouts, and simple image edits. Outputs can look templated if you accept the first suggestion, and fine-grained control is limited — treat it as a fast start, then adjust. Check whether you qualify for education-plan benefits before paying for features.

How to combine them (without burning prep time)

  • Plan: draft the lesson skeleton in ChatGPT; edit it against your standards yourself.
  • Present: paste the outline into Gamma, fix the examples, done in minutes.
  • Research: use Perplexity for background facts and click through the citations.
  • Publish: finish handouts and visuals in Canva.
  • Recover time: let Otter handle meeting notes so evenings stay yours.

Never feed identifiable student data into consumer AI tools. Strip names, IDs, and anything covered by your district's privacy policies first.

A note on academic integrity

These same tools are available to your students, which is exactly why your classroom AI policy matters more than any detection app. Practical approaches that work: be explicit about when AI use is allowed and when it isn't; design assignments that require personal context, in-class thinking, or process evidence (drafts, outlines, annotations); and teach citation of AI assistance the way you'd teach citing any source. Detection tools are unreliable enough that false accusations are a real risk — treat them as a signal to start a conversation, never as proof.

Frequently asked questions

Is the free tier enough?

For typical weekly use, mostly yes. Heavy users hit caps on assistants and transcription minutes. Limits change frequently — verify on each tool's pricing page (ChatGPT, Gamma, Otter.ai, Perplexity, Canva AI).

Which single tool should I start with?

One general assistant. Master prompting there first; the specialized tools amplify a skill you already have, not replace it.

Do these tools train on my inputs?

Policies vary by tool and plan. Review each provider's data-use terms and privacy settings before pasting anything sensitive.
