    Handing off an AI-assisted client deliverable

    Keep the client-facing artifact defensible: source its claims, review its rights and promises, and send a file the client can actually use.

    Quick answer

    AI can help draft a client deliverable, but the handoff is your work. Agree what the client is receiving, keep their source material within approved systems, trace every factual statement to a document you opened, and review any promised result or usage right before sending. Deliver an editable file with the decisions, open questions, and owner of the next step visible. A polished draft without those checks is not finished work.

    Start with a handoff contract, not a prompt

    Write down the actual artifact: a brief, a deck, a summary, or a recommendation memo. State who will use it, what source materials are in scope, which facts are still disputed, and whether the client expects a decision or just options. An assistant cannot settle a missing scope agreement; it will fill the gap with a plausible structure. If the deliverable is advice in a regulated field, obtain the appropriate qualified review instead of treating the model as the professional.

    Keep a small source register beside the draft: document title, owner, date received, permission to use it, and the section where it supports a claim. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. When a client asks where a line came from, a link back to their document is better than a second model's confident paraphrase.

    Prepare the inputs and permission boundary

    Separate public materials from confidential briefs. Remove unnecessary personal details and do not paste client files into a personal AI account because its interface is convenient. Check the client's contract, your internal policy, the vendor's data-use terms, and the sharing settings first. Our data-connection checklist covers access, retention, and exit questions. If the terms cannot be confirmed, use redacted excerpts or do the sensitive part manually.

    For a text draft, Claude or ChatGPT may help reorganize approved notes. For a visual handoff, Canva AI or Gamma may help lay out material. Those are options, not endorsements for confidential work: access controls, export formats, and rights terms must be evaluated against your own requirement.

    Draft in layers the client can challenge

    Ask for an outline first. Map every section to its approved input, then draft only the sections that have enough material. Mark an unsupported recommendation as a question for the client rather than letting the model supply an imaginary number or customer quote. If a chart is needed, prepare its underlying data separately and check the arithmetic yourself. Presentation software can arrange a chart; it does not make the underlying figures true.

    Put the decision up front and the evidence beside it. A client should not need to infer which points came from their brief, which are your analysis, and which are proposed next steps. Avoid a generic “AI generated” disclaimer as a substitute for that separation. The proposal-response workflow shows why scope and commitments need a named owner rather than a fluent draft.

    Run three reviews before the send

    1. Evidence: open each cited source; check names, dates, quotations, calculations, and whether a qualifier was lost. Remove any statement whose support you cannot locate.
    2. Commercial and rights: review promises, timelines, confidentiality, image and audio licenses, and any attribution the contract requires. A vendor's export button is not permission to use its output in every context. See the commercial image-rights checklist.
    3. Usability: open the exported file, verify links and layout, confirm the client can edit what they need, and remove private comments, hidden slides, and stray internal notes.

    A second assistant can enumerate claims for review but should not certify them. The draft-review checklist gives the full fact-and-commitment pass. Keep the source register internal unless the client wants it, but make the final's references readable enough for a human to follow.

    What belongs in the handoff note

    Send the final artifact, the editable version if promised, a short list of decisions required, and the limits of the analysis. Say which inputs were used and the date they were current. If a fact depends on an unconfirmed vendor plan, note that plans change and point to the tool review or official source for current terms. Do not silently convert a draft recommendation into a contractual commitment.

    End with one named owner and a next action. Avoid implying continued support or implementation unless it is explicitly in the agreed scope. The handoff is complete when the recipient can understand the recommendation, inspect its basis, and tell what remains theirs to decide.

    When to leave AI out

    Skip AI when consent is absent, the source set is too sensitive for the available environment, or the work requires a professional judgment you cannot independently make. A manual page with reliable facts beats a polished but unverifiable deliverable. Do not buy another subscription just for a one-off handoff; use the tool you already govern, or none.

    Frequently asked questions

    Should I tell a client AI helped with the draft?

    Follow the contract and the client's disclosure policy, and be honest if asked. Do not claim a human wrote a passage that was only generated. More important, identify who checked the facts and owns the final advice.

    Can a model check the finished deliverable for me?

    It can flag possible omissions and collect claims into a review list. It cannot establish that a cited document says what the draft claims, nor approve a commercial promise. A responsible person must open the sources and approve the send.

    What if I cannot verify a source before the deadline?

    Remove the unsupported statement or mark it clearly as an open question. Do not hide the gap in a footer. A short, qualified deliverable is more useful than a complete-looking one that cannot withstand a client's follow-up.
