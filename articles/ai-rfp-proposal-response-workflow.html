    Workflow guide

    An AI-assisted RFP and proposal response workflow

    Reuse what you already wrote, draft one requirement at a time, and keep every commitment with a person.

    Quick answer

    Proposal work is mostly retrieval: the same capability statements, security answers, and case-study skeletons, restated for a new evaluator. That is the part AI handles well — finding what you already wrote and reshaping it to the question asked. It is the wrong tool for what loses bids: delivery commitments, prices, certifications you do not hold, references you have not re-cleared. The model restates approved material; a person owns every commitment.

    What this workflow does not do

    It does not decide whether to bid. A model will draft a confident response to a requirement you cannot meet, and a polished document hides that better than a blank page. Keep bid/no-bid human: do you meet the mandatory requirements, can you staff the dates, is the scoring weighted toward anything you can evidence.

    Assemble three reference piles first

    Drafting quality is decided before the first prompt, by what the model has to work from.

    • Approved past responses. Two or three submissions that scored well, with feedback if you have it. Strip client names where an NDA applies.
    • Facts you can cite. Security posture, certifications, uptime, team biographies, and the wording legal will stand behind. Every claim traces to a source inside your organisation, which is what keeps drafting honest.
    • The question set. The RFP split so each requirement is its own item. Drafting per requirement beats drafting per document — a model given forty questions answers the general shape of all of them.

    Step one: a reusable answer library

    If you respond more than a few times a year, the reference pile should become a library: one entry per recurring question, with an approved answer, its evidence, an owner, and a date. Same discipline as a team prompt library applied to one document type; Notion is a reasonable home because entries carry permissions and a review date. The honest cost is upkeep: libraries go stale where it matters — certifications lapse, headcount changes, an integration is deprecated. A stale answer is worse than none, because it gets reused unchecked.

    Step two: draft per requirement

    Work one item at a time: the question, the closest approved answer, the evidence line, and the length the RFP specifies. Ask it to flag anything the material does not support instead of filling the gap — a model asked to answer will answer, and the gap gets papered over in fluent prose.

    Long question sets are where Claude and ChatGPT differ in feel rather than in kind — one holds a long instruction set more steadily, the other rewrites more fluidly. Neither removes the read-through, and both will invent a certification number if the document's pattern invites one. For reading-heavy material, a source-grounded tool such as NotebookLM fits better: it answers against the documents you loaded and cites the passage — a pointer to read, not proof.

    Step three: the compliance pass

    Before anyone edits for style, check the response against the requirement list mechanically: every question answered, in the order asked, with the format and page limits the buyer set. This catches the deadline failure — a good answer to the wrong question, or a blank mandatory form. A table works better than a model here: one row per requirement, a column for where the answer lives, a column for evidence attached.

    Then read the draft aloud, or have someone who did not write it read it cold. Two things surface: sentences that promise more than you meant, and numbers that drifted between sections. Dates, headcount, uptime, and prices are where drafting errors become contractual.

    What should not go into the model

    Proposal material is commercially sensitive in both directions. Keep out the buyer's confidential annexes unless your agreement permits it, other clients' case studies without permission, internal pricing and margin, staff personal data, and anything under an NDA. A de-identified version with the same structure teaches the shape without the exposure.

    Check the training and retention terms for your tier before loading anything sensitive. Whether AI tools are safe for business data covers those questions, and the vendor questionnaire is what you send when a buyer asks the same of you.

    A first pass in ninety minutes

    1. Pick the two best past responses and note which sections scored well.
    2. List the ten questions that recur in your market and write the approved answer to each.
    3. Draft the current RFP one item at a time, flagging gaps rather than filling them.

    Before you buy anything for this

    None of this needs a proposal-specific product on day one: an assistant plan you already pay for covers drafting, and a document tool you own covers the library. Add a dedicated platform when upkeep is the bottleneck — one used four times a year is a subscription you cancel.     Frequently asked questions

    Can AI write an entire RFP response on its own?

    Not one you should submit. It can draft from approved material and flag gaps, but commitments, prices, and certifications have to come from a person who can be held to them. Treat output as a draft for review.

    Is it safe to upload a buyer's RFP into an AI tool?

    Only if your agreement with that buyer allows it, and only on a tier whose retention terms you have read. Many RFPs carry confidentiality clauses. When in doubt, work from a retyped question list.

    What is the biggest risk in AI-assisted proposals?

    Plausible invention. A model asked to answer a requirement will produce a fluent answer even when the supporting fact is missing, and fluent prose is harder to challenge in review than a blank. Ask it to flag unsupported gaps.
