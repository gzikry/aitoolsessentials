What agencies actually get wrong about AI seats

Solo buyers optimise for capability. Agencies have to optimise for four things the consumer plans do not address: seat economics, permission boundaries, brand consistency across writers, and the right to hand output to a client. A tool that is excellent for one person often becomes a liability at eight, because the per-seat price is only the visible cost.

The hidden costs are the ones that show up in month two: seats you bought for contractors who finished their engagement, a plan tier that silently excludes the feature your senior writer needs, and output you cannot legally deliver because the plan you are on reserves commercial rights for a higher tier.

Do the seat math before you buy

A worked example using the verified numbers, for a seven-person agency with three writers, two designers, one producer, and one account lead:

  • Writing layer (3 seats). Jasper Pro at $59/seat/mo billed annually is $177/month. Claude Team at $20/seat/mo is $60/month for the same three people. The difference is roughly $117/month — about $1,400/year — and it buys brand-voice controls rather than raw writing quality.
  • Creative layer (2 seats). Firefly Pro at $19.99/seat/mo is $39.98/month for 4,000 credits per seat. Credits, not seats, are the real constraint for image-heavy work.
  • Meetings layer (7 seats). Fireflies Pro at $10/seat/mo billed annually is $70/month across the whole team. This is the cheapest per-seat layer and usually the easiest to justify.
  • Producer and account lead. Do not buy them a writing seat. Give them the shared assistant and the meeting tool; a seat they open twice a month is the most common wasted line on an agency's AI invoice.

Total for that shape lands near $290-350/month depending on which writing path you take. The single biggest lever is the writing layer, and the decision there is brand consistency versus cost.

Permissions, client data, and what you are allowed to deliver

Two questions decide most agency rollouts, and neither is answered by a feature comparison.

Who can see what? Shared workspaces are the reason agencies move off individual plans, but a shared workspace also means a junior can read a senior's client drafts. Check whether the plan separates projects or workspaces per client before you consolidate everyone onto one account.

Can you deliver the output? Commercial-use rights are plan-specific. On Claude, the Consumer Terms govern Free and Pro while Team and Enterprise add commercial terms. On Jasper, delivery rights sit in the main Terms. On Adobe Firefly, the generative-AI user guidelines are what make it the safer default for client-facing visuals. The practical rule: read the rights page for the exact tier you are buying, and do it before the first client deliverable, not after.

Meeting recording is a consent problem, not a tooling problem. Any tool that joins a client call — Fireflies included — captures client conversation. A written recording policy that you state in the call is a prerequisite. This is the one area where a cheap seat can create an expensive conversation.

Rollout order that survives month two

  1. Start with the meeting layer. It is the cheapest per seat, the benefit is obvious to everyone in the room, and it produces a searchable record that improves every other workflow.
  2. Add one assistant for the whole team before adding specialists. A shared assistant replaces scattered personal subscriptions and gives you one place to answer the data-handling question.
  3. Add the writing layer only when brand consistency is a real problem. If two writers produce inconsistent client copy, brand-voice tooling earns its premium. If they do not, you are paying $1,400/year for a feature you will not use.
  4. Add creative generation last. Image and video credits are the hardest to forecast, and the rights question is the most consequential because the output is most visible to clients.

Agency AI questions we get asked

Should an agency buy team plans or individual subscriptions?

Team plans, once you pass roughly three people. Individual subscriptions cannot be administered — you cannot offboard a departing employee, you cannot enforce a permission boundary per client, and the data-handling terms are written for a consumer. The visible per-seat premium is usually smaller than the administrative cost of not having it.

How do we stop paying for seats nobody uses?

Audit seats monthly against actual logins, not against who asked for one. Contractors and part-time contributors are the usual source of waste — buy them seats for the engagement and remove them when it ends. Most vendors bill per active seat, so an unused seat is a pure loss.

Which tools can we safely use on client work?

Start with the rights page for the tier you are on, not the marketing page. Firefly is the safest default for client-facing visuals because Adobe publishes explicit generative-AI guidelines. For writing, Team and Enterprise tiers generally carry commercial terms that consumer plans do not. Never assume a consumer plan you tested personally grants delivery rights at work.

Do we need to tell clients we use AI?

Increasingly yes, and it is usually contractual rather than optional. Check your master service agreements for AI disclosure clauses, and state your recording policy in the call before any meeting tool joins. Being able to describe your process plainly is a sales advantage with sophisticated clients, not a risk.
