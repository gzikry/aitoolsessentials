    Pricing question

    What are AI credits?

    The unit AI pricing actually runs on — and how to convert it into a monthly number before you subscribe.

    Editorial focus: how credit pricing works in general, not a rating of any single plan. Plan structures and limits change — verify current details on each vendor's official pages or in our tool reviews.

    Quick answer

    An AI credit is a prepaid unit of consumption. Instead of — or in addition to — paying for a seat, you buy an allotment of credits, and every action the tool performs spends some number of them at a rate the vendor sets: generating an image, a few seconds of video, a passage of speech, an agent run. When the credits are gone, the plan is done until they refresh or you buy more. Flat pricing pays for access; credit pricing pays for usage. The practical consequence: two credit plans cannot be compared on headline numbers, because what a credit buys differs in every tool.

    Why vendors bill in credits

    Compute costs vary enormously per action. A short chat reply costs a vendor almost nothing; a minute of generated video is expensive. A flat subscription has to price for the heavy users and lose money on them, or price high and lose the light ones. Credits solve that by charging for the expensive thing directly. They also hand the vendor two quiet levers: steering, where cheap actions run on a basic model and premium models cost more per run, and flexibility, where an action's credit cost can change without the plan's sticker price changing. Neither lever is hidden malice — but both mean the plan you bought can buy less next quarter than this quarter.

    What one credit actually buys

    There is no standard unit. Across the tools in this directory, a credit can stand for an image generation, a few seconds of video, a block of synthesized speech, or time on a shared GPU queue — image tools like Midjourney and Leonardo AI meter GPU-time-style units, a voice tool like ElevenLabs meters characters of speech, a video tool like Runway meters generations or seconds, and an aggregator like Poe spends points at different rates per underlying model. The burn also changes with settings: higher resolution, faster priority queues, and premium models typically cost more credits per action than the defaults a marketing page shows. Plan structures change, so treat any neat one-to-one conversion as dated and verify on the vendor's current pricing page before you budget from it.

    Allotments, rollover, and top-ups

    Three mechanics decide what an allotment is really worth. First, the reset: monthly allotments usually refresh on your billing date, and unused credits often expire — a generous monthly allowance is worth little to spiky usage if nothing carries over. Second, rollover: some tools let unused credits carry forward for a limited window, and that often matters more than the headline number when your workload comes in bursts. Third, top-ups: extra credit packs sold mid-cycle, sometimes priced differently from plan credits and sometimes with their own expiry. Also check what happens to remaining credits when you downgrade or cancel — in many tools they are simply gone, which is an argument for burning the allotment before the switch, not after.

    Seats plus credits

    Watch for tools that charge both meters at once: a per-seat fee for access plus a credit allowance that heavy features draw down quickly. Avatar and video tools are the usual shape — a tool like HeyGen or Synthesia bundles seats and minutes together. Compared against a flat-priced tool, the flat tool looks expensive until you price the credits you would actually burn.

    Estimate your real burn

    The only reliable estimate comes from your own workload. On a free tier or the cheapest paid tier, run one representative week of real work and count finished outputs, not attempts — the drafts you discarded spent credits too. Note the quality settings you actually needed, since higher settings multiply the per-action cost. Multiply the week into a month, add headroom for busy stretches, and then pick the plan whose allotment covers that number rather than the one whose example scenario looks cheapest. Getting real value from free trials is the structured version of exactly this exercise.

    Compare across tools: cost per usable output

    Cross-tool comparison needs one common number: cost per finished, usable output — one deliverable image, one minute of final video, one finished narration, one completed agent task. Marketing pages quote different units precisely so you cannot line them up; converting is your job. If a tool cannot tell you plainly what an action costs on the plan you are considering, that is an answer about the tool.     Frequently asked questions

    Do unused AI credits roll over to the next month?

    It depends entirely on the tool. Some monthly allotments reset to zero, some roll over for a limited period, and top-up packs often outlive the subscription credits that came with the plan. Check the rollover line on the vendor's pricing page before assuming a large allowance is usable.

    Why did the same action cost more credits this month?

    Credit tables change. Vendors reprice actions, move models between tiers, and adjust quality defaults — that flexibility is the point of credit pricing. If your burn changes unexpectedly, check the current rate card on the vendor's official page before assuming something went wrong with your usage.

    Is a credit plan ever better than a flat subscription?

    Yes, when usage is genuinely spiky. One heavy week a month can be cheaper on an allotment than on a flat seat, while steady daily usage usually favors flat pricing. Estimate your real monthly burn first, and the answer follows from the number rather than from the marketing page.
