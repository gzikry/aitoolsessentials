    Reviewing AI drafts before they ship

    A tiered review pass that spends attention on the sentences that can cost you money, not the ones that merely read awkwardly.

    Quick answer

    Reviewing an AI draft is not proofreading. Sort it into three tiers by what a mistake would cost: facts, numbers, and quotes get checked line by line against a source you opened yourself; commitments get a named human decision; voice gets a fast read. AI can help you find the claims worth checking, but it cannot be the authority on whether they are true.

    Why "give it a check over" fails

    A bad AI draft rarely looks bad. It looks finished: clean headings, confident transitions, a number exactly where a number should go. The failure mode is plausibility, so review effort has to scale with the cost of being wrong rather than with how polished the draft appears. A blog post that overstates a feature is an edit. A proposal that misstates a delivery window is a renegotiation.

    One general pass over the whole document is the wrong shape. The sentences that matter are a small fraction of the word count, and they deserve most of the attention.

    Tier one: facts, numbers, and quotes

    Start by making the claims visible. Ask the drafting tool for a numbered list of every verifiable claim — figures, dates, names, capabilities, quotations — and to mark which ones it can support from material you supplied. Treat that list as an index, not as evidence.

    • Numbers. Recalculate what you can and open a source for the rest. Models are unreliable at arithmetic even when the reasoning sounds right.
    • Quotations. Never accept one you have not copied from the source. The common failure is a real sentence attached to the wrong person or year.
    • Names. Companies rebrand, merge, and rename products. A name that reads current can be a year behind.
    • Capability claims. "Supports X" is a factual statement about a product that changes with every release. Check the vendor page or the tool's review page.

    A second model can help: paste in the draft plus your sources and ask it to list claims the sources do not support. That catches omissions. It is a cross-check rather than verification, and it will confidently approve things both models are wrong about.

    Tier two: commitments

    Prices, discounts, delivery dates, scope, service levels, and regulated advice cannot be delegated to a draft. If generated text promises something the business has not approved, better prompting is not the fix — an approval is. Keep one named person responsible for the sentences that could be quoted back at you in a dispute, and make it a rule that nothing reaches a customer without that person reading it.

    Tier three: voice and the tells readers notice

    Read the draft aloud. Repeated sentence shapes, stacked hedges, and paragraphs that restate their own headings are what make a page feel generated, and they are easier to hear than to see. A style checker such as Grammarly is useful for consistency and plain language. It cannot tell you whether a sentence is true.

    While you are there, check the claims the writer makes about itself. "We" sentences drift: a draft will describe a process the team does not run or a policy nobody wrote. Superlatives and unqualified guarantees are usually worth deleting rather than editing.

    Put the review before the share

    The gate that works is unglamorous: draft, claim list, source check, human sign-off, publish. Reviews that start after a draft is circulated turn into corrections, and corrections cost more attention than the original review would have taken. Assembly can be automated — an automation in Make or Zapier can pull the draft into a review document, attach the claim list, and assign the sign-off. The signature stays human, because no automation can tell a well-sourced sentence from a confident one.

    The ten-minute pass

    1. Every figure has a source you opened this week, or the sentence is cut.
    2. Every quotation matches the source, with the right speaker and date.
    3. Product and company names are current.
    4. No capability claim rests on the model's memory.
    5. No price, date, or scope commitment appears without an owner's approval.
    6. Nothing asserts a test, benchmark, or measurement the page cannot show.
    7. Links open and point at the page you meant to cite.
    8. A person has read it end to end and will put their name on it.

    When you cannot verify a claim

    You have three honest options: cut it, attribute it, or hedge it in a way the reader can see. Attribution is usually best — "the vendor's documentation lists X" is checkable, while "X is true" is not. A disclaimer is not on the list. It does not make an inaccurate sentence acceptable, and readers read it as an excuse.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can AI fact-check another AI's draft?

    Partly. A second model is good at surfacing claims that lack support in text you supply, which saves time building the list. It cannot confirm a claim against the outside world, and two models can share the same wrong assumption.

    How much of an AI draft should I rewrite?

    Rewrite whatever you would be uncomfortable defending: the opening, any claim you could not source, and every sentence that sounds like a brochure. The middle often needs less work than the framing does.

    Does a disclaimer protect an inaccurate AI draft?

    No. A note that content may be inaccurate does not fix a wrong price, a misattributed quote, or a promise nobody made. The correction has to happen in the sentence, not in a footer.
